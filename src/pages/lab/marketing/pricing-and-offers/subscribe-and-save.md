# Subscribe & Save

Subscribe & Save turns one-time purchases into subscriptions. Customers get a discount in exchange for automatic repeat delivery or billing. Amazon made the name familiar, and many direct-to-consumer brands now use it. It makes revenue more predictable and raises customer lifetime value.

## How it works

A customer who buys coffee every six weeks can forget, delay, or switch brands. A subscriber's next order is already scheduled. The discount is paid for by lower reacquisition cost and higher lifetime value. Customers get convenience and savings. The business gets forecastable revenue and better inventory planning. Not reordering takes no effort. Canceling takes a decision.

## How to do it

1. Pick products with regular usage cycles: coffee, supplements, pet food, razors, skincare, cleaning supplies.
2. Find the median time between repeat purchases. Make that the default delivery interval, with shorter and longer options.
3. Set a discount that is meaningful but sustainable. Start around 10-15% and test other levels with new customers.
4. Build a self-service portal where subscribers can skip, change frequency, swap products, and cancel without contacting support.
5. Send a reminder a few days before each shipment with links to skip, edit, or add items.
6. Send a win-back email about a week after cancellation with a small incentive to restart.

## What to measure

- **Subscription adoption rate**: subscription orders divided by orders on eligible products.
- **Subscriber retention**: share of subscribers still active at 3, 6, and 12 months.
- **Subscriber LTV vs. one-time LTV**: average lifetime revenue of subscribers compared with one-time buyers of the same products.

## Best practices

- Test making subscription the default choice on the product page, with one-time purchase as the alternative.
- Let subscribers choose items for each delivery. Active choice keeps them engaged.
- Let customers skip a delivery as easily as they can cancel. Many who would cancel will skip instead.

## Common pitfalls

- Making cancellation hard. Hidden cancel buttons and required phone calls cause complaints, bad reviews, and regulatory risk.
- Ignoring cancellation reasons. "Too much product" means the default interval is too short. Survey cancellers and act on patterns.
- Launching without the basics: a management portal, flexible schedules, failed-payment retries, and dunning emails. A half-built program creates more support work than revenue.

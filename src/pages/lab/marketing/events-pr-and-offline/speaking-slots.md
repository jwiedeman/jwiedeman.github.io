# Speaking Slots

Speaking at industry conferences puts your experts in front of a room of likely buyers. The audience chose to attend, and a good talk earns a level of trust that ads cannot. Treat speaking as a pipeline channel, with targets and follow-up, rather than as a vanity item.

## How it works

People attend conference sessions because they want to solve a problem. A speaker who addresses that problem with real insight, data, and practical frameworks earns credibility, and the audience links that expertise to the speaker's company. It builds over time: past talks make it easier to get future slots, each talk produces reusable content, and conversations at the event become sales relationships in the following months.

## How to do it

1. List 15 to 25 conferences your buyers attend. Rank them by audience fit, not size.
2. Note each call-for-proposals deadline, often months before the event.
3. Submit several proposals per event, each with a specific title, a clear takeaway, and a distinct angle. Include the speaker bio, past talks, and any original data.
4. Build the talk around two or three core insights backed by data and customer examples. Mention your company once, briefly.
5. Include one framework, visual, or data point people will remember.
6. Rehearse until delivery is smooth but natural.
7. Announce the talk a few weeks before the event and have sales book meetings with target accounts who are attending.
8. Record the talk. Turn it into a blog post, short clips, and a webinar replay, and send it to prospects who missed it.

## What to measure

- **Post-event pipeline**: value of qualified pipeline from contacts met or influenced at the event, within 90 days.
- **Audience engagement**: number of attendees who approach the speaker, connect online, or visit your booth afterward.
- **Recording reach**: views, downloads, and shares of the recording and derived content.

## Best practices

- Submit early and offer more than one proposal so organizers have options.
- Build relationships with organizers between events by attending, moderating, or reviewing proposals.
- End with a useful takeaway, such as a checklist or template, and offer to send it by email.
- Follow up with each meaningful contact within 48 hours, referring to your actual conversation.

## Common pitfalls

- A sales pitch dressed as a talk. Audiences notice quickly and stop listening.
- Choosing events for prestige instead of audience fit.
- No follow-up plan. The talk opens doors, but pipeline comes from follow-up.

# Social Proof

Social proof is the tendency to treat what others do as a guide to what is right. When prospects see that many companies use your product, that a respected peer recommends it, or that reviews are strong, choosing you feels less risky. It is one of the easiest trust signals to add at scale.

## How it works

When people are unsure, they look at what others did and follow. Social proof moves the work of evaluating from the individual to the crowd. The closer the reference group is to the prospect (same industry, role, or problem), the stronger the effect. That is why a testimonial from a peer CMO persuades an enterprise buyer more than a generic "10,000 customers" badge.

## How to do it

1. Find the pages where prospects feel the most doubt. Pricing, checkout, and signup forms usually come first.
2. Collect five types of proof: customer counts, logos, specific testimonials, case studies with results, and third-party ratings (G2, Trustpilot, and similar).
3. Match proof to funnel stage: logos and counts early, case studies in the middle, peer testimonials at the decision point.
4. Where it is true and current, show live proof, such as recent purchases or new reviews.
5. Review your proof every quarter and replace anything outdated.

## What to measure

- **Conversion lift from proof**: A/B test pages with and without social proof elements.
- **Decision drivers**: ask new customers in a post-purchase survey what influenced their choice.
- **Review velocity**: count new reviews collected each month.

## Best practices

- Use testimonials with specific, real outcomes rather than vague praise like "great product."
- Segment proof by audience. Show SaaS testimonials to SaaS prospects and ecommerce case studies to ecommerce buyers.
- Place proof at points of friction, not only in the hero section. A testimonial on the checkout page can matter more than one on the homepage.

## Common pitfalls

- Using fake reviews. They destroy trust when found and can lead to FTC action.
- Showing impressive but irrelevant logos. Fortune 500 logos can put off small-business buyers.
- Relying only on big numbers ("1 million users!") without the detail that makes them believable.

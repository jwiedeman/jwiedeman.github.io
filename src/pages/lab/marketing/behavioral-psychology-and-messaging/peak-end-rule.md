# Peak-End Rule

The peak-end rule, described by Daniel Kahneman, says people judge an experience mostly by two moments: the most intense point (the peak) and the end. Duration and average quality matter much less. An average experience with one great moment and a strong ending is often remembered better than a consistently good one that ends badly.

## How it works

Memory keeps snapshots, not a full recording. When people look back, they rely on the peak and the ending and largely ignore the rest. A flight with turbulence in the middle but a smooth landing and a warm goodbye can be remembered better than a fine flight with a rough landing. For customer experience, this means investing heavily in one memorable peak and a strong ending.

## How to do it

1. Map the customer journey. Mark the current peak (best moment) and the end (final interaction).
2. Design one deliberate peak that beats expectations: a surprise gift, a personal onboarding video, an unexpected upgrade, or a handwritten note.
3. Design strong endings: thank-you sequences after purchase, friendly confirmation pages, and offboarding that leaves the door open.
4. Find and fix the worst moments. A bad support call can become the peak that defines the relationship.
5. Apply the rule to single touchpoints. End emails with the strongest line, pages with the strongest element, and sales calls with a clear value statement.

## What to measure

- **NPS by journey stage**: survey at each stage and compare scores around the designed peak and ending.
- **Unprompted recall**: in interviews, ask customers what they remember and note which moments come up.
- **Repeat purchase rate**: compare return rates for customers who experienced the designed peak and those who did not.

## Best practices

- Make one moment excellent rather than every moment slightly better.
- Fix bad endings before adding new peaks. A bad ending can erase a good peak.
- Make cancellations, returns, and contract endings gracious. People who leave on a good note come back.

## Common pitfalls

- Spreading effort evenly so the experience is fine but forgettable.
- Investing only in the first impression and neglecting renewals and cancellations.
- Creating a peak unrelated to the core product. A great unboxing does not save a disappointing product.

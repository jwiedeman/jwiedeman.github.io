# Loss Aversion

Loss aversion is the bias, described by Kahneman and Tversky, where losing something hurts roughly twice as much as gaining the same thing feels good. In marketing, showing what prospects lose by not acting often motivates more than showing what they gain. It underlies free trials, money-back guarantees, and "don't miss out" messages.

## How it works

People weigh losses and gains unevenly. Losing $100 feels worse than gaining $100 feels good. So a message about avoiding a loss ("Stop losing revenue to abandoned carts") often beats the same message framed as a gain ("Recover more revenue"). It also explains why free trials work: once users have the product, taking it away feels like a loss.

## How to do it

1. Review your headlines and CTAs. Write a loss-framed version of each ("Stop wasting $X on...") and test it against the gain version ("Save $X with...").
2. Design trials and free tiers so users build habits, create data, and customize settings. Losing access should then feel like a real loss.
3. Add a cost-of-inaction calculator that shows the prospect what the problem costs them each month.
4. In cancellation flows, list exactly what the user will lose: specific features, saved data, and history.
5. Offer a money-back guarantee so the risk sits with inaction, not the purchase.

## What to measure

- **Loss vs gain test lift**: A/B test loss-framed and gain-framed versions and compare conversion rates.
- **Trial-to-paid conversion rate**: the share of trial users who become paying customers.
- **Cancellation save rate**: the percentage of users who start to cancel but stay after seeing what they would lose.

## Best practices

- Use loss framing for big decisions where prospects tend to stall and do nothing.
- Make the loss specific and personal: "You will lose your 14 saved workflows and 6 months of history" beats "You will lose premium features."
- Pair loss framing with a real deadline so people do not deliberate forever.

## Common pitfalls

- Overusing fear. It creates anxiety and bad feelings about the brand.
- Making exaggerated loss claims. "You'll fall behind your competitors forever" gets eye-rolls.
- Framing inaction as a loss when the product cannot actually prevent that loss.

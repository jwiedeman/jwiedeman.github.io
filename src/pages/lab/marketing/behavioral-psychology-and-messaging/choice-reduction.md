# Choice Reduction

Choice reduction (also called the paradox of choice, after Barry Schwartz) is the finding that fewer options often lead to more purchases and happier customers. In Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper's jam study, shoppers who saw 6 jams were far more likely to buy than shoppers who saw 24. Too many options stall decisions instead of helping them.

## How it works

Every extra option has to be evaluated, compared, and traded off. When there are too many, the effort becomes overwhelming and the buyer takes the easiest path, which is usually doing nothing. Choice reduction removes low-value options, curates what remains, and gives a clear recommendation so the decision feels manageable.

## How to do it

1. Review your catalog, pricing tiers, and feature lists. Find places where buyers face more options than they can reasonably compare.
2. Cut pricing to three tiers at most. Mark one as recommended with a label such as "Most Popular."
3. Add guided selling: a quiz, needs assessment, or recommendation tool that narrows options before showing them.
4. Remove products that serve the same need at a similar price.
5. Show the top few options first and put the rest behind a "See all options" link.

## What to measure

- **Decision time**: measure time from first viewing the options to making a selection.
- **Conversion rate by option count**: A/B test pages that show different numbers of choices.
- **Post-purchase satisfaction**: survey buyers with CSAT or NPS and compare by how many options they saw.

## Best practices

- Always show a default recommendation. Most people will take the suggested option.
- Group options into categories instead of one long flat list.
- Keep configuration light before purchase. Let people customize after they buy.

## Common pitfalls

- Cutting so many options that the offer looks thin instead of curated.
- Labeling an option "recommended" without saying why.
- Giving every visitor the same choice set. New visitors need more curation than returning power users.

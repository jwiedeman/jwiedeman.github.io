# Consistency & Commitment

The consistency principle says that once people take a small action or make a statement, they feel pressure to act in line with it. In marketing, getting a prospect to take one small step (answer a quiz, use a free tool, agree to a minor request) makes the next, larger step more likely. This is the logic behind most nurture sequences.

## How it works

People want to look consistent with what they have already done and said. A voluntary action shifts how they see themselves: "I am someone who does X." Later requests that fit that identity are easier to accept. This is one reason trial users convert better than cold prospects: buying is consistent with having signed up. The effect is strongest when the first commitment is active, public, and voluntary.

## How to do it

1. Map a ladder of 4 to 6 asks, each slightly larger than the last. Start with something almost effortless, such as one quiz question or a single email field.
2. Make the first ask active and personal. "Tell us your biggest marketing challenge" binds more than "Enter your email."
3. Reference earlier actions in follow-ups: "Since you downloaded our SEO guide, you may want..."
4. Collect profile data across several visits instead of in one long form.
5. Where it fits, add public commitments: community introductions, shared goals, or visible progress milestones.

## What to measure

- **Step-through rate**: the percentage of users who complete each step of the ladder.
- **Free-to-paid conversion rate**: the share of free or trial users who become paying customers.
- **Engagement depth**: the number of small commitments a user made before buying.

## Best practices

- Make the first ask give the user something useful, not just collect data.
- Connect each step clearly to the last one and to the user's stated goal.
- Write CTAs in the user's voice. "Start my free audit" frames the action as their choice; "Submit" does not.

## Common pitfalls

- Asking for too much too early. Requiring a credit card for a "free" trial triggers pushback.
- Ignoring earlier commitments in follow-up messages.
- Bait-and-switch escalation that asks for more without giving more. It destroys trust once people notice.

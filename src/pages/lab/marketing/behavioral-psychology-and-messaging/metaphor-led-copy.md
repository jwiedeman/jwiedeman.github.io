# Metaphor-Led Copy

Metaphor-led copy explains abstract or technical ideas by comparing them to something the audience already understands. When Slack calls itself "your digital HQ," or a security company promises "a firewall for your data," readers understand the new thing through a familiar one. Metaphors do more than describe. They shape how people think about and judge the product.

## How it works

People understand new ideas by mapping them onto ideas they already have. Linguists George Lakoff and Mark Johnson argued that metaphor is central to how people think, not decoration. Calling a product "the Swiss Army knife of marketing" carries many associations at once (versatile, compact, reliable) without listing them. A good metaphor can say in a few words what a feature list takes paragraphs to explain.

## How to do it

1. List the parts of your product that are hardest to explain, such as data pipelines, algorithmic optimization, or workflow automation.
2. Match each one to a familiar domain: navigation (map, compass), construction (foundation, blueprint), health (check-up, diagnosis), or everyday tools (remote control, dashboard).
3. Test each metaphor with real prospects to confirm the associations are accurate and positive.
4. Choose one central metaphor and use it consistently. If the product is a "command center," features are "controls" and insights are "radar."
5. Put the metaphor in headlines and opening lines. Follow it with a literal explanation in the body copy.

## What to measure

- **Comprehension time**: in user tests, time how long it takes people to explain the value proposition with metaphor copy versus literal copy.
- **Ad recall**: measure recall in brand lift studies for metaphor-led and literal ads.
- **Explain-back accuracy**: ask prospects to describe the product in their own words and score how accurate they are.

## Best practices

- Pick metaphors from the audience's world, not yours. A farming metaphor suits agricultural buyers, not startup founders.
- Do not mix metaphors. A product cannot be both a "rocket ship" and a "safety net."
- Prefer concrete images. "A flashlight in a dark room" is easier to picture than "a paradigm shift in visibility."

## Common pitfalls

- Using worn-out phrases such as "cutting-edge," "game-changer," and "seamless." They no longer carry meaning.
- Picking a metaphor with unwanted side meanings. "Walled garden" suggests lock-in.
- Stretching a metaphor until it breaks. "Cockpit" works for "controls," not for "crash landing."

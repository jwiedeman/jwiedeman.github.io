# Brand Mascot & Character

A brand mascot is a character that represents the brand: an animal, a person, a creature, or an object with a face. Examples include the Geico Gecko, Mailchimp's Freddie, Duolingo's Duo, and the Michelin Man. Mascots are most useful in categories where the product itself carries little emotion, such as insurance, email software, or language learning.

## How it works

People form attachments to characters, including fictional ones. A mascot gives the brand a face and a personality, which makes it easier to remember and talk about. It also solves a content problem. Screenshots get stale and stock photos look generic, but a mascot can be placed in new situations indefinitely: seasonal campaigns, social replies, error pages, onboarding, and merchandise.

## How to do it

1. Define the mascot's role. Options include spokesperson (the Gecko delivers the pitch), helper (Clippy guided Office users), personality carrier (Duo), or product metaphor (the Energizer Bunny stands for battery life).
2. Write a character brief. List 3-5 personality traits, the visual style, the range of expressions, and the relationship to the audience (mentor, friend, sidekick). Add a list of things the mascot never does.
3. Hire a designer to produce the character in several expressions (happy, surprised, thinking, celebrating, confused) and formats (full body, head, silhouette, icon). Check that it still reads at favicon size. Deliver vector files.
4. Roll it out in stages. Start with high-exposure spots: website hero, 404 page, loading states, social profile, welcome emails, and in-product empty states.
5. Plan ongoing use: recurring social posts, seasonal illustrations, small product animations, and merchandise. Use one illustrator or a shared design system so the character looks the same everywhere.

## What to measure

- **Recognition rate**: share of surveyed target buyers who name your brand when shown the mascot without the logo.
- **Engagement difference**: engagement rate on posts with the mascot versus without, compared over a rolling 3-month window.
- **Affinity score**: survey ratings of how friendly or approachable the brand seems, taken before and after launch.

## Best practices

- Give the mascot a name and a short backstory. Freddie is a chimp who works in email. Duo is an owl who wants you to practice. This gives writers a consistent character to write for.
- Allow quirks. Duolingo leaned into the meme of Duo's pushy reminders instead of fighting it, and the character became more shareable.
- Design for animation from the start, even if you only use still images at first. Define key poses and expressions in the brief.

## Common pitfalls

- Looking like every other mascot in the category. If competitors already use friendly animals, choose a different art style or character type.
- Launching without a long-term plan. A mascot that appears in one campaign and then disappears is wasted. Budget for ongoing illustration.
- Designing by committee. Stacked requests ("friendlier," "more professional," "add a hat") produce a bland character. Give one strong designer the brief, then refine.

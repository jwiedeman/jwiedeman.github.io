# Value Proposition Hierarchy

A value proposition hierarchy ranks what your product offers: one main promise at the top, a few supporting benefits below it, and specific proof under each. Without it, messaging splits. The website tells one story, sales tells another, and ads tell a third. The hierarchy keeps every touchpoint on the same promise, with more or less detail depending on context.

## How it works

There are three levels. The primary value proposition is the one promise that matters most to your target customer, in one sentence. Under it sit 3-4 pillars, each a distinct benefit that supports the promise. Under each pillar sit features, numbers, and customer evidence that make it believable. This matches how buyers read: the promise tells them whether to care, the pillars explain how, and the proof makes them believe it.

## How to do it

1. Draft 5-8 candidate promises from customer research (interviews, win/loss notes, support tickets). Use the form: "We help [customer] achieve [outcome] by [mechanism]." Lead with outcomes, not features.
2. Test the candidates with ad or landing page tests, or a preference survey of target prospects. Pick the one that tests well and that your product can actually deliver.
3. Group your benefits into 3-4 pillars that each support the promise. For a hiring tool promising faster hires: automated sourcing, candidate screening and ranking, interview scheduling, and offer analytics. Each pillar must be distinct, not a restatement of the promise.
4. Under each pillar, list 2-4 proof points: specific features, measured results from your own customers, and quotes.
5. Put the hierarchy on one page and share it with marketing, sales, product, and customer success. Note where each level is used: the promise in homepage heroes and ad headlines, pillars on product pages and sales decks, proof in case studies and docs.

## What to measure

- **Message consistency**: share of customer-facing assets that use the documented promise and pillars, audited quarterly.
- **Promise recall**: share of prospects who can restate your main promise, from post-exposure surveys or sales call notes.
- **Pillar engagement**: page views, demo requests, and sales-call questions per pillar, used to decide whether to reorder them.

## Best practices

- Keep it to 3-4 pillars. More than that and the audience has to decide what matters for you.
- Write the main promise in plain, simple language. A prospect should get it in a few seconds. Technical detail belongs in the proof layer.
- Change the emphasis by audience, not the structure. Enterprise buyers may care most about security; small businesses may care most about setup time.

## Common pitfalls

- Making the promise a feature. "AI-powered analytics platform" describes a product. "Know which marketing dollar drives revenue" states an outcome.
- Giving every stakeholder's favorite feature equal billing. The point is ranking. One owner, such as the CMO, makes the final call.
- Treating it as permanent. Review it twice a year and after any major launch, competitive move, or new research.

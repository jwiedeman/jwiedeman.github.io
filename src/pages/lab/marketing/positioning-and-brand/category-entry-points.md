# Category Entry Points

Category Entry Points (CEPs) are the situations and needs that make a buyer think of a product category. "We keep missing deadlines" is an entry point for project management software. A water stain on the ceiling is an entry point for roofing services. The Ehrenberg-Bass Institute popularized the idea. Its point is simple: a brand linked to more buying situations gets considered more often, and consideration comes before purchase.

## How it works

Buyers do not think about your brand. They think about their problem, and their memory offers up whichever brands are linked to that situation. CEPs are the doors into your category. The more relevant doors your brand is linked to, the more often it comes to mind when someone is ready to buy. Brand building should therefore strengthen links to a wide set of real buying situations, not repeat one message.

## How to do it

1. List every situation, need, occasion, and motivation that could trigger your category. For meal delivery: "too tired to cook," "want to eat healthy with no time," "hosting friends last-minute," "just moved to a new city." Aim for 15-25.
2. Survey category buyers. For each CEP, ask how often they face that situation and which brands come to mind. Ask unaided first, then aided.
3. Plot each CEP on a grid: how common the situation is versus how strongly buyers link it to your brand. Common and strong: protect. Common and weak: growth opportunity. Rare and strong: niche. Rare and weak: ignore for now.
4. Pick 3-5 common CEPs where competitors beat you. Build campaigns that tie your brand to those exact situations.
5. Write the situation into everyday marketing. Instead of "Our meal kits use fresh ingredients," write "Too tired to cook tonight? Dinner is ready in 15 minutes."

## What to measure

- **Mental share per CEP**: share of surveyed buyers who name your brand unaided for each entry point, tracked yearly.
- **CEP breadth**: number of entry points where your brand passes a set recall threshold (for example, named by 10% of respondents), compared with competitors.
- **Link to consideration**: compare how many CEPs a respondent links to you with whether they would consider you.

## Best practices

- Show the situation before the product in ads. "You just realized there is nothing for dinner" builds a stronger link than a feature list.
- Measure CEPs separately from awareness. People can know your brand and still not think of it when the need arises.
- Refresh the map every year. Behavior shifts create new entry points; remote work, for example, created new ones for collaboration tools.

## Common pitfalls

- Spending everything on one CEP. The link gets strong but narrow, and competitors win the other situations.
- Confusing CEPs with audience segments. "Marketing managers at mid-size companies" is a segment. "I need to prove ROI to my boss by Friday" is an entry point.
- Trusting internal assumptions. Run the survey. The situation you spend most on is often not the most common one.

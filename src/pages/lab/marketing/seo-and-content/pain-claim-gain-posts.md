# Pain-Claim-Gain Content Formula

Pain-Claim-Gain is a simple structure for persuasive content. First, describe the reader's problem clearly. Second, state a specific claim that a better outcome is possible. Third, prove the gain with evidence. It works for blog posts, landing pages, emails, and video scripts.

## How it works

Most people read marketing content because they have a problem. Naming the problem in their words shows you understand it. The claim gives them a reason to keep reading. The evidence makes the claim believable. Each part needs the others: pain without a claim is just complaining, and a claim without proof is just a promise.

## How to do it

1. Collect real pain points from support tickets, sales call notes, reviews (yours and competitors'), forums, and "People Also Ask" results.
2. Write the pain section in the audience's own words and make it specific. "You spent $3,000 on ads this month and can't tell which campaigns drove revenue" is better than "Marketing is hard."
3. Write one sentence stating the claim. Make it specific and believable, for example "You can cut weekly reporting from four hours to fifteen minutes."
4. Prove the claim. Use before-and-after numbers, a named customer example, screenshots or a walkthrough, testimonials, or a step-by-step explanation of why it works.
5. Connect the proof to your next step. Show how your product or offer produces the result, without an abrupt hard sell.
6. Turn the structure into a content brief template with three fields: Pain (with source quotes), Claim (one sentence), Gain (evidence and source). Use it for every piece.

## What to measure

- **Scroll depth by section**: share of readers who reach the claim and the gain sections, from scroll tracking or heatmaps.
- **Conversion rate**: share of readers who take the target action, compared with pages that do not use the structure.
- **Engaged time**: average engagement time on the page, compared with similar content.

## Best practices

- Quote real phrases from customers in the pain section.
- Make the claim specific and measurable where you can back it up.
- Put your strongest evidence first in the gain section, since many readers will not read every proof point.

## Common pitfalls

- Dwelling on the pain. Keep it short and move to the claim quickly.
- Making claims you cannot support. A modest claim with strong proof beats a bold claim with weak proof.
- Using vague pain points. "Running a business is hard" speaks to no one in particular.

# Data Studies for Link Building

A data study is original research you publish so others can cite it. Journalists, bloggers, and analysts need numbers to support their stories. When you are the original source of a useful number, they link to you. These links are citations, not favors, so they are earned without the usual outreach trade.

## How it works

Writers cite sources. Most companies do not produce original data, so the few that do receive a large share of citations. A study works when it gives other writers a specific number, trend, or finding they want to quote. The more useful and credible the finding, the more often it is linked.

## How to do it

1. Find a data source only you have, such as anonymized product usage, transaction trends, or customer benchmarks. If you have none, run a survey or analyze public data such as job postings, government datasets, or company filings.
2. Pick a topic that interests your audience and the journalists who cover your industry. Reliable formats are annual "state of" reports, benchmarks by industry, year-over-year trends, and rankings.
3. Run the analysis and list 5-10 findings. Choose the one or two most surprising or useful as headline findings.
4. Publish on a dedicated web page, not only a PDF. Include a methodology section, charts for each key finding, and a summary with the headline numbers in the first paragraph.
5. Offer embeddable charts with an attribution link back to the study.
6. Build a list of journalists and writers who have cited similar studies. Send each a short, personal pitch with the finding most relevant to their beat.
7. Post individual findings on social channels and pitch newsletter editors and podcast hosts during the first few weeks after launch.

## What to measure

- **Linking domains**: unique sites linking to the study page, tracked monthly in a backlink tool.
- **Press mentions**: articles that cite the study, found with brand and study-name alerts.
- **Ongoing citation rate**: new linking domains per month after the launch period ends.

## Best practices

- Publish the same study every year. Repeat reports become expected, and journalists start looking for them.
- Include shareable charts and single-stat images that are easy to drop into another article.
- State the sample size, data source, dates, and methods clearly so writers can cite you with confidence.

## Common pitfalls

- Choosing a topic only existing customers care about. Findings need to interest a wider group of writers.
- Hiding the main finding in a long PDF. Put the key numbers at the top of the page.
- Skipping promotion. Few studies get found on their own; outreach drives most early links.
